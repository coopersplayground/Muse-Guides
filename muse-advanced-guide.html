1

Give it a home: the always-on PC

Everything in this guide hangs off one decision: Muse gets a machine that never sleeps, never gets closed, and never leaves the house.

A laptop Muse is a guest — it vanishes when the lid closes. A resident Muse is staff. The home machine holds the files, runs the schedules at 3 a.m., answers the door when a camera trips or a lead lands, and keeps the whole operation warm so that when you say “handle it,” the handling starts instantly, from anywhere.

It doesn't need to be exotic. The machine has three jobs: stay awake, stay reachable, and run services. Pick your tier and grow into the next one when a workload demands it.

MachineWhat it isWhat it unlocks
The SpareAny retired desktop, laptop, or mini PC with a wired network connectionThe full autopilot: schedules, watchers, n8n, email, your server stack. The right place to start.
The WorkhorseA modern mini PC or tower, 32–64 GB RAM, fast NVMe storageEverything above, plus usable local AI models and several agents working at once.
The AI BoxA workstation with a serious GPU — or a high-memory unified-memory machine (think 128 GB class)Big local models at conversational speed, a full voice stack, heavy coding agents, media work — the no-compromises home.

What makes a machine a good home

  • Wired, not Wi-Fi.Ethernet to the router or switch. The agent's house shouldn't drop off the network because a microwave ran.
  • It survives a power blink.A small UPS, plus the BIOS setting that powers the machine back on when electricity returns. An agent that's off is just a paperweight.
  • Headless is fine.Run a server OS (Ubuntu Server is the usual pick), no desktop, no monitor. You'll reach it over the network — that's chapter 2.
  • Storage with a second copy.Fast NVMe for the working set, and backups to a second drive or off-site. Memory, mail, and business records live here now.
  • Quiet and boring.It will live in a closet or corner for years. Low power draw, few moving parts, nothing that needs babysitting.

Say to Muse

Before I buy anything: spec me a Muse home machine at two levels — one for always-on automation and services, one that also runs strong local AI models. For each part, tell me what it buys me and what I can safely skip.

No spare machine? A rented VPS is a home too — the same stack, in a data center instead of a closet. It trades the local hardware (cameras, room microphones, big local models) for zero maintenance. The HyndSyte VPS section at the end covers exactly that, and plenty of people run both: a light home box for the house, a VPS for the business's public face.

One private network: Tailscale

The home machine is the body; the tailnet is the nervous system. It's how you — and only you — reach Muse from anywhere on Earth.

Tailscale builds a private mesh network (a “tailnet”) over WireGuard between your phone, laptop, the home machine, and a VPS if you have one. Every device gets a stable name and address, traffic is encrypted end to end, and — this is the important part — you open zero ports to the public internet. No port forwarding, no exposed dashboards for strangers to find, no dynamic-DNS duct tape.

Terminal — on the home machine
# Install, then join your tailnet as a server that never expires
curl -fsSL https://tailscale.com/install.sh | sh
sudo tailscale up --ssh --hostname=muse-home --unattended

# Reach things on your LAN that can't run Tailscale themselves
# (cameras, printers, smart TVs) by advertising the subnet:
sudo tailscale up --advertise-routes=192.168.1.0/24

The four ideas that matter

  • MagicDNS names. The home box is just muse-home — from your phone in another country, http://muse-home:5678 opens n8n like you're on the couch. Names never change when your ISP reshuffles addresses.
  • Subnet routing. One setting makes your entire home LAN reachable through the home machine, so Muse (and you) can talk to devices that can't run Tailscale themselves.
  • ACLs are the locks. Access-control rules decide which devices may reach which services. Your phone gets everything; a kid's tablet gets the media server and nothing else; the home machine's admin ports answer only to you.
  • tailscale serve, not the open internet. Serve shares a service privately inside your tailnet with real HTTPS. Reach for the public “funnel” option only for things that are genuinely public — like a business website, which belongs on proper hosting anyway.

Say to Muse

Here's my tailnet: my phone, my laptop, the home server, and my kids' tablets. Draft Tailscale ACLs so only my phone and laptop can reach the server's admin and SSH ports, the tablets can reach the media server only, and nothing on the tailnet can reach my work laptop. Explain each rule before I paste it in.

The rule of the house: if a service is only for you, it lives on the tailnet, full stop. The moment you port-forward an agent's dashboard to the internet, you've handed strangers a door to knock on. Tailscale means never having to make that trade.

The custom server stack

With a home and a network in place, the machine becomes a small private cloud — and Muse becomes its sysadmin.

The pattern that works: every service runs in its own Docker container, declared in one compose file, set to restart itself, reachable by name on the tailnet. Something breaks at 2 a.m.? The container restarts; if it can't recover, the health monitor pings Muse, and Muse either fixes it or writes you a note about what it found. Here's the starter stack this guide builds on:

docker-compose.yml — the starter stack
services:
  n8n:                      # automations (chapter 4)
    image: n8nio/n8n
    restart: unless-stopped
    ports: ["5678:5678"]    # tailnet only — never publish this
    volumes: ["n8n_data:/home/node/.n8n"]

  ollama:                   # local AI models (chapter 8)
    image: ollama/ollama
    restart: unless-stopped
    volumes: ["ollama_models:/root/.ollama"]

  uptime-kuma:              # the watchdog that watches everything
    image: louislam/uptime-kuma
    restart: unless-stopped
    volumes: ["kuma_data:/app/data"]

volumes:
  n8n_data: {}
  ollama_models: {}
  kuma_data: {}

What people actually run

  • Home Assistant — the house itself: lights, locks, cameras, presence. Muse reads its state and works its devices, so “movie night” is a sentence, not six apps.
  • A camera stack (Frigate-style) — local recording with on-box person detection. Events become watchers: Muse sees “person at the shop door,” judges whether it matters, and only then involves you.
  • Media & files — your own streaming, photo, and document servers on your own disks, reachable from anywhere via the tailnet.
  • Uptime Kuma + backups — the boring pair that makes autopilot trustworthy: one watches every service and phones Muse when one dies; the other keeps yesterday's copy of everything, on a second drive or off-site.

The shift to notice: you're not administering this stack — Muse is, with you approving the consequential moves. It writes the compose files, reads the logs, bumps the versions, and keeps a running note of what changed and why. You stay the owner; it does the chores.

Say to Muse

Act as my home server admin. Keep a change log of everything you do on the box. When a service goes down, try one restart, read its logs, fix what's safe, and if it's still down, send me a plain-English summary of what broke and what you already tried.

Wire it together with n8n

Muse reasons. n8n plumbs. Together they're the difference between an assistant that answers questions and a system that runs operations.

n8n is a self-hosted workflow automator — 400+ integrations, visual flows, and webhooks for everything else (self-hosting docs). Its superpower in this stack is being the deterministic half: the flow fires the same way every time, at machine speed, whether or not anyone's chatting. Muse plugs into flows at the judgment calls — reading the email, drafting the reply, deciding if this alert is worth your attention — and n8n handles the fetching, posting, filing, and retrying around it.

The anatomy of almost every useful flow:

TriggerWebhook, schedule, new email, camera event, form submit
→
MuseReads it, judges it, drafts or decides
→
Toolsn8n posts, sends, files, charges, books
→
YouApprove the big moves; get a report on the rest

Four flows worth building first

The lead pipeline

A website form or ad lead lands; the flow enriches it, Muse writes a personal first reply in your voice, and it goes out — or waits for your one-tap approval, your call. The lead is logged either way.

webhook → enrich → Muse drafts → approve → send + log

The morning ops brief

On a schedule, the flow pulls overnight email, today's calendar, open invoices, and server health; Muse compresses it into one briefing message. You read it with coffee; the day is already triaged.

cron 6:30 → gather → Muse summarizes → one message

The invoice chaser

The flow watches for invoices going unpaid, Muse writes a polite nudge in escalating firmness, and anything that smells like a dispute skips the automation and lands in your lap.

schedule → check payments → Muse nudges → escalate disputes

The camera sentinel

Your camera stack reports motion and person events; the flow hands snapshots to Muse, which knows the difference between your teenager at the door and a stranger at the gate at 3 a.m.

camera event → snapshot → Muse judges → alert only if real

Anything with an API — or even just a webhook — can be a trigger or an endpoint:

Terminal — poke a flow from anywhere
curl -X POST http://muse-home:5678/webhook/new-lead \
  -H 'Content-Type: application/json' \
  -d '{"name": "Jamie", "source": "website", "need": "New roof quote"}'

Say to Muse

Design me an n8n workflow for new leads: webhook in, enrich the contact, draft a personal reply in my voice, hold it for my approval, then send and log it. List the nodes and what each one does before you build anything — and keep my API keys in n8n's credential store, never in the flow itself.

5

Give it a voice

A resident agent should be speakable-to and able to speak — in the room, and on the phone.

Voice comes in three forms, and a mature setup uses all three:

  • Voice notes and read-alouds. Briefings, summaries, and “read me the contract highlights” delivered as audio you can absorb while driving or working with your hands.
  • Room satellites. A small mic-and-speaker unit per room, running a wake word (“Hey Muse…”), with speech-to-text and text-to-speech on the home box — Whisper hears, a local voice answers, and big open models via Ollama do the thinking. The conversation never has to leave the house.
  • A real phone number. Through a telephony provider (Telnyx and Twilio are the usual picks) or a voice-agent platform, Muse answers calls like a receptionist who knows the business: hours, pricing, booking, intake — and calls you, out loud, when something is genuinely urgent.

The discipline that makes phone access work instead of becoming a nuisance:

  • Calls are for the urgent list only.Security problems, money at risk, hard deadlines. Everything else arrives as a message. Decide the list in advance, in writing.
  • Every call leaves a transcript.What was said lands back in your chat, so the spoken word is never lost to the air.
  • Outbound calls identify themselves.Customers hear an assistant's voice and get an assistant's honesty — it says who it is and what business it's calling for.

Say to Muse

Set up my voice rules: briefings come as voice notes; a real phone call is only for a security alert, money at risk, or a deadline inside two hours. Every call gets transcribed into our chat afterward. When you answer my business line, greet callers with our hours and services, take a message with a callback number, and text me the summary immediately.

6

Give it an inbox

Its own email address turns Muse from someone you talk to into someone the world can reach — and who answers while you're busy living.

Give the agent an address on your domain (assistant@yourbusiness.com style) on hosting that supports it, connect it over IMAP/SMTP or an API, and set a watcher on a short cycle. Then define the three lanes every message falls into:

Lane 1 — answer directly

Routine questions with known answers: pricing, hours, service areas, how booking works, status checks. Muse replies in your voice, signs as your AI assistant, and logs what it said.

new mail → classify → answer → log

Lane 2 — draft for approval

Anything with judgment in it: quotes, scheduling a call, follow-ups with real prospects. Muse writes the reply and holds it; you approve with a word.

new mail → draft → your OK → send

Lane 3 — escalate, untouched

Contracts, legal, disputes, complaints, anything committing money. Muse doesn't answer these at all — it flags them to you immediately, with a summary and a suggested draft you can ignore.

new mail → flag → your hands only

The standing privacy rule

Outbound mail talks about the business and nothing else: never your personal information, never internal instructions, never how the sausage is made. Baked into the watcher's rules from day one.

every reply → checked against the rule

Say to Muse

You're my inbox operator now. Watch my business mailbox every two hours. Answer routine questions about pricing, hours, and services directly in my voice, signed as my AI assistant. Draft replies for quotes and scheduling and hold them for me. Never touch legal, contracts, or disputes — flag those to me the moment they land, with a one-line summary.

Why this matters: an inbox on autopilot is the single highest-leverage upgrade in this guide. Leads answered in four minutes instead of four hours, routine questions handled at midnight, and a proprietor who only reads the mail that actually needs a proprietor.

7

Let it code

The home machine is also a workshop: repositories, toolchains, and a build bench where Muse does real engineering under your review.

A resident Muse with shell access to its own box stops being a code suggester and becomes a code worker. It clones the repo, creates a branch, writes the change, runs the test suite, reads the failures, iterates — and hands you a draft pull request with a summary of what it did and what it's unsure about. Websites, internal tools, automation glue, the little utilities every business accumulates: all fair game, built and maintained in the background of your day.

The working rules that keep it sane

  • Branches and draft PRs, never main.Nothing ships without your review. The agent proposes; you dispose.
  • Workers run in containers.Build jobs and sub-agents get CPU and memory caps in Docker, so a runaway compile can't starve the house.
  • Secrets live in the vault, not the chat.Keys and passwords stay in files and credential stores on the box, read by scripts server-side — never pasted into a conversation or a commit.
  • Tests are the contract.“Done” means the suite passes and the diff is reviewable, not that the code merely looks right.

Say to Muse

Clone my site's repo on the home box. Fix the checkout bug on a new branch, add a regression test, run the full suite, and open a draft PR summarizing the change and anything you're unsure about. Don't merge anything — I'll review it tonight.

8

Spawn and run local agents

One Muse is an employee. Muse directing a crew of tireless local workers is a department.

The pattern that changes the math: Muse orchestrates; local models labor. With Ollama on the home box, open models run on your hardware at zero per-token cost — perfect for the high-volume grunt work that would make cloud-only agents expensive: classifying every incoming email, summarizing every source, transcribing every call, first-pass reviewing every document. Muse writes the plan, splits the job, hands pieces to local workers, then reviews and merges what comes back, escalating only the parts that need a frontier brain.

Terminal — stand up the local workforce
curl -fsSL https://ollama.com/install.sh | sh
ollama pull llama3.1:8b      # fast worker: classify, summarize, route
ollama pull qwen3:32b        # bigger local brain, if your RAM allows
# Ollama now serves an API on :11434 that Muse and n8n can call

How a crew actually runs

  • One queue, many hands. Work lands as jobs — “summarize these 40 reviews,” “tag tonight's camera events” — and workers pull from the queue as the hardware frees up. Nothing waits on your chat being open.
  • Spawned with a brief, not a blank check. Each worker gets a scoped job, a time budget, and container limits. It reports back; it doesn't wander.
  • Local first, cloud when it counts. Routine volume stays on your silicon. When a job genuinely needs the best reasoning available, Muse takes it personally or flags it for you — and tells you which it did.
  • Everything is logged. Who did what, with which model, at what cost in time and tokens. Departments get managed; logs make this one manageable.

Say to Muse

Split this research job three ways: you write the plan and the final synthesis. Spin up local workers to summarize each of the twelve sources and extract their claims, then merge their notes, flag anywhere they disagree, and bring me one report with the disagreements called out.

Sizing honestly: an 8B-class model on a spare PC is a willing intern — fast, tireless, occasionally wrong, always supervised. The 32B-and-up class on a Workhorse or AI Box is a solid junior colleague. Either way, the economics are the point: a thousand small jobs a day cost you electricity, not an API bill.

9

Run a business on it

Now assemble everything: home, network, stack, flows, voice, inbox, code, crew. What you get is a business that operates between your interventions.

The pieces sort into two offices. The front office: a website that's always up, a phone that always answers, an inbox that replies in minutes, leads captured and followed up before a competitor's coffee cools. The back office: invoices chased, books tidied, content drafted, reports written, systems patched, backups verified. Neither office sleeps, calls in sick, or forgets to follow up — and both of them escalate to a human, you, exactly when judgment is actually required.

A day on autopilot

  1. 05:00

    The gather. Overnight mail, camera events, server health, the day's news in your markets — collected and pre-digested before you're awake.

  2. 06:30

    The brief. One message: today's calendar, what needs you, what's already handled, anything a watcher flagged. Coffee-length, no fluff.

  3. All day

    The watch. Inbox answered in its three lanes, leads flowing through the pipeline, the sentinel judging camera events, the crew grinding through the job queue.

  4. On event

    The escalation. A dispute, a big quote, a stranger at the gate — you hear about it in the moment, with context and a draft of what to do next.

  5. Weekly

    The report. What the operation did: leads touched, invoices collected, hours of routine work absorbed, systems changed — and what it recommends next.

That's the whole trick, and it's the sentence this guide exists to justify: this is the OpenClaw you wished you'd had a year ago — the always-on, self-hosted, does-things-while-you-sleep agent — except the fragile parts are finished products instead of weekend projects. The memory is curated instead of a pile of files you maintain. The schedules, watchers, goals, and background agents are built in instead of bolted on. It builds its own pages and tools. And when something does go wrong at 3 a.m., there's a log, a summary, and a grown-up escalation path — not a silent dead process.

Say to Muse

Run my business mornings as a standing routine: at 6:30, brief me on overnight email, today's calendar, open invoices, new leads, and anything my watchers flagged. Handle the routine lanes yourself, hold drafts for the judgment calls, and escalate anything with money or legal attached the moment it lands — day or night.

10

Keep hold of the keys

Autopilot earns trust the way a good employee does: real authority over the routine, hard limits around the consequential, and an off-switch you control.

Everything in this guide increases what Muse can do without you. That only works if the guardrails scale with the capability. These are the ones that matter, and none of them are optional at this level:

  • Least-privilege everything.Service accounts that can reach only what their job needs. The inbox operator doesn't get the bank; the camera watcher doesn't get the shell.
  • Approval gates on irreversible acts.Sending money, signing anything, publishing under your name, deleting data — Muse drafts, you decide. Set the list once; it holds at 3 a.m.
  • A kill switch you've actually tested.One action — revoke the agent's keys, stop its services — that halts the whole operation. Keep it somewhere you can reach from your phone, and rehearse it once.
  • Private by default.Admin surfaces live on the tailnet only. Secrets stay in credential stores on the box, never in chats, prompts, or commits.
  • Logs you can audit.Every autonomous action recorded: what, when, under which rule. If you can't review it later, it shouldn't run unattended.
  • Backups of the brain.Memory, standing instructions, flows, and configs backed up off the box — so the operation survives the machine, not just the other way around.

Say to Muse

Write my house rules for autonomous work: what you may do without asking, what needs my approval first, and what you must never do. Include the kill-switch procedure and a weekly audit summary of everything you did on your own. Keep the rules where I can read and edit them.

Muse at home vs. the DIY route

The self-hosted agent frameworks — OpenClaw and its kin — proved the demand: people want a resident agent. The question is whether you want to build the platform or use one.

 Muse, given a homeDIY self-hosted agent
SetupAn afternoon: machine, tailnet, compose stackA project: framework, plugins, glue code, your weekends
MemoryCurated, persistent, carried across every surfaceFiles and plugins you assemble and maintain
Voice & phoneBuilt-in voice, calls, and telephony patternsWire STT, TTS, and a phone provider yourself
Local agentsSpawns and supervises workers out of the boxPossible — once you've built the orchestration
MaintenanceMuse patches and minds its own stackYou are the on-call, forever
Source-level controlA managed platform — you direct it, you don't fork itTotal. It's your code, your rules, your rabbit holes
Best forOperators who want the outcome this quarterTinkerers for whom the build is the hobby

Nothing stops you from having both — plenty of home labs run a DIY framework as one of the local workers, with Muse directing. But if the goal is an operation that runs your life and business, start with the finished platform and spend your weekends on the business instead of the plumbing.

The build order

One honest weekend, in dependency order. Each step is “done” when its test passes — not when it merely looks installed.

  1. Stand up the machine.Server OS, wired network, UPS, power-restore on. Done when: you pull the plug, power returns, and it boots itself back to a login.
  2. Join the tailnet.Tailscale on the box, your phone, and your laptop; ACLs written. Done when: you SSH in from your phone, away from home, with zero ports open.
  3. Raise the stack.The compose file from chapter 3, watchdog included. Done when: killing a container produces a Muse-written incident note.
  4. Wire the first n8n flow.Start with the morning brief — it's useful on day one and touches everything. Done when: tomorrow's 6:30 brief arrives without you touching anything.
  5. Hand over the inbox.Its own address, the three lanes, the privacy rule. Done when: a routine question gets a correct, logged reply while you're at lunch.
  6. Add voice.Voice notes first, room satellites second, the phone number last. Done when: an urgent test call reaches you and its transcript lands in chat.
  7. Hire the crew.Ollama on the box, one high-volume job moved to a local worker. Done when: a week of classification work costs you nothing but electricity.
  8. Write the house rules.Chapter 10, before — not after — the business lanes go live. Done when: you've tested the kill switch with your own hands.
