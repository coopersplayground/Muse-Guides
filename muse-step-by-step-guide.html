Step 1

Pick the box — the hardware Muse will live on

Muse itself runs in the cloud, but the moment you want it to hold files, run servers, watch cameras, or keep working while your laptop sleeps, it needs a home: an always-on machine on your network. You do not need a supercomputer. You need uptime, storage, and enough RAM for the jobs you hand it.

TierWhat it isGood forRough cost
StarterA retired desktop or mini-PC you already own, 16 GB RAM, any SSDFile storage, n8n automations, a personal dashboard, learning the ropes$0–150
WorkhorseModern mini-PC or workstation, 32–64 GB RAM, 1–2 TB NVMeEverything above plus Docker stacks, local AI models (Ollama), media, multiple websites$400–900
Homelab serverDedicated server-class box, 64 GB+ RAM, mirrored storageBusiness operations: hosting client sites, camera systems, voice stack, local agents at scale$1,000+

The five buying rules

  • RAM beats CPU. Containers, databases, and local models all eat memory first. 32 GB is the sweet spot to grow into.
  • SSD or nothing. Spinning drives make everything — including Muse's patience — slower. NVMe if the board allows it.
  • It must run 24/7 quietly. If it sounds like a hair dryer, you'll unplug it, and autopilot dies with it.
  • Put it on a UPS. A $60 battery backup turns a power flicker from a corrupted database into a non-event. Set the BIOS to Restore on AC Power Loss → Power On so the whole stack resurrects itself.
  • Wire it, don't Wi-Fi it. Ethernet to the router or switch. Servers on Wi-Fi are a troubleshooting hobby you didn't ask for.

Operating system

Install Ubuntu Server LTS (or Ubuntu Desktop if you want a GUI for the first week). Linux is where Docker, Node, and every tool in this guide assume you live. During install: enable OpenSSH, create your user, and write the machine's local IP down — you'll reserve it in your router so it never drifts.

I'm setting up a home machine for you to work on. It's Ubuntu Server with [RAM] and [storage]. Walk me through, one step at a time: reserving a fixed IP on my router, enabling SSH, and the first security updates. Explain each command before I run it.

  • ✓Ubuntu installed, OpenSSH enabled
  • ✓Fixed/reserved IP set in the router
  • ✓BIOS set to power back on after power loss
Step 2

The first conversation — what to actually ask Muse

Most people waste their first week with a new AI asking it trivia. The highest-value first move is an intake interview: let Muse ask you the questions, then store the answers. Ten minutes here saves you re-explaining yourself forever.

Before we do anything else, interview me. Ask me about my work, my family, my schedule, how I like answers formatted, what I never want you to do without asking, and what matters most this month. Ask a few questions at a time, and when we're done, save a summary as my standing profile and read it back to me for corrections.

Then set the house rules, in plain English

These are my standing rules — save them and follow them always: (1) Ask before sending anything to another person. (2) Questions are questions — if I ask something, answer it, don't go do things. (3) Show me a plan before any change that costs money or can't be undone. (4) Keep answers short unless I ask for depth. Confirm you've saved each rule.

Five first-week prompts that pay for themselves

  1. “Here's my week — find the conflicts.” Paste your calendar or describe it; Muse spots double-bookings and prep gaps.
  2. “Turn this brain-dump into a plan.” Talk messy for two minutes; get back an ordered list with owners and dates.
  3. “What should I know about X before my meeting?” Real research with sources, not vibes.
  4. “Draft it in my voice.” Give one past email as a sample; every future draft starts from your tone.
  5. “Remind me every weekday at 8 to…” Your first scheduled job — the moment Muse stops being a chatbot and starts being staff.
The golden rule of askingState the outcome, the constraints, and the finish line: “Write a quote email for a bathroom remodel, under $150 words, friendly but firm on the deposit, and show me the draft — don't send it.” Vague in, vague out.

  • ✓Standing house rules saved
  • ✓First scheduled reminder created
Step 3

Teach it your world — memory, people, projects

Muse's memory is layered: a curated profile of durable facts, plus daily notes. You build it by correcting it, not by writing essays. Whenever it gets something wrong, say so once — “My daughter's school is Fairview, not Crestwood” — and ask it to save the correction.

  • People: names, relationships, schools, birthdays, how to reach them. Muse keeps a page per person and stops mixing them up.
  • Projects & businesses: what each venture does, its pricing, its rules (“never discount the annual plan”). Business rules saved once are quoted back in every draft.
  • Preferences: food, tone, tools, “don't ever do X.” Preferences are the cheapest quality upgrade there is.
  • Corrections compound: a month of small corrections produces an assistant that sounds like it's worked for you for years — because, in memory terms, it has.
Show me everything you currently have saved about me, my family, and my businesses, grouped by topic. I'll correct anything wrong line by line — update your memory as we go.
Privacy postureMemory lives in your Muse workspace, and you can inspect or delete any of it on request: “Forget everything about X.” Never dictate passwords, card numbers, or government IDs into memory — credentials belong in a password vault (Step 6), never in chat or notes.
  • ✓People pages started for household/family
  • ✓Business facts and pricing saved
  • ✓Reviewed the full memory readout once
Step 4

Connect accounts and load skills

Out of the box Muse can talk. Connected, it can do: read your email, run your calendar, search your files. Connect in this order — each unlocks the next:

  1. Email (Gmail or your business mailbox) — triage, drafting, follow-up tracking.
  2. Calendar — scheduling, conflict checks, morning briefings that know your day.
  3. Drive / Docs — research and writing grounded in your real documents.
  4. Business tools as needed — Stripe for payments, Shopify for a store, social accounts for posting.

Skills are reusable playbooks — flight booking, inbox management, shopping, trip planning, home control, and dozens more. You don't install them like apps; you trigger them by asking for the outcome (“find me a flight to…”). Ask Muse what's available for a job before assuming it can't.

Which of my accounts are connected right now, and what can you actually do with each? Then tell me the three connections that would save me the most time based on my profile, and walk me through connecting the first one.
  • ✓Email connected and a test triage run
  • ✓Calendar connected; tomorrow's day summarized
  • ✓One business tool connected (Stripe, Shopify, or social)
Step 5

“Program it all” — with Muse, in plain English

Here's the mental shift: you are not writing code, you are specifying behavior. Muse writes the code, runs it, tests it, and shows you the result. Your job is the same as managing a good developer: clear spec, review the demo, request changes in words.

The build loop that works

  1. Describe the outcome, not the implementation. “A page that shows today's jobs and lets me tap one to text the customer directions” — not “a React app with a REST API.”
  2. Ask for a plan first on anything big. Data it needs, screens, what could go wrong. Approve the plan, then say “build it.”
  3. Review the working thing, not the code. Muse builds pages you can open, tap, and break. Your feedback is about behavior: “the total is wrong when tax is added.”
  4. Iterate in small bites. One change per message lands cleanly; ten changes in one message land eventually, with confusion.
I want a simple tool that [describe the outcome]. Before building: give me a short plan — what it will do, what information it needs from me, and what it will NOT do. I'll approve the plan, then you build a first version I can open and test.

What Muse can build for you

  • Pages & dashboards — guides like this one, quote calculators, status boards, family command centers.
  • Automations & scripts — rename 400 photos, reconcile a spreadsheet against invoices, watch a folder and file things.
  • Real apps — with the server from Step 7 or HyndSyte hosting (Step 8): booking systems, client portals, stores, internal tools with logins and databases.
  • Documents & media — PDFs, spreadsheets, narrated videos, images — produced from your data, not templates.
Say this when stuck“Explain what you built like I'm the customer, not the programmer — what do I click, what happens, and what happens if something goes wrong?” If the explanation doesn't match your mental picture, the build doesn't either. Fix the picture first.

  • ✓One change requested and landed cleanly
  • ✓You can explain what it does without reading code
Step 6

SSH & keys — giving Muse hands on your machines, safely

SSH is the remote control for servers. A key pair replaces passwords: the private key stays on the machine that connects from, the public key goes on the server. No password ever travels, and there's nothing to phish or brute-force once password login is turned off.

Generate and install a key (you run this once)

ssh-keygen -t ed25519 -C "muse-access" ssh-copy-id youruser@192.168.50.50 ssh youruser@192.168.50.50

Then harden the server

sudo nano /etc/ssh/sshd_config # set: PasswordAuthentication no # set: PermitRootLogin no sudo systemctl restart ssh

Before restarting SSH with password login off, keep your current session open and test the key in a second window. Locking yourself out is a rite of passage you can skip.

The safety rules that make this sane

  • Dedicated user, not root. Muse works as its own user (e.g. cosmo) with sudo only where needed — every action is attributable and revocable.
  • Keys live on machines, never in chat. Never paste a private key or password into any chat, ever. If a setup needs a secret, it gets typed on the machine itself or stored in its config file with locked-down permissions.
  • Keep a kill switch. One command (or one edited file) that revokes Muse's access: remove its public key from ~/.ssh/authorized_keys, or disable its user. Know where it is before you need it.
  • Reach home from anywhere via Tailscale (Step 7) instead of opening SSH ports to the internet.
I want you to be able to work on my home server over SSH as a dedicated user — not root, no password login. Give me the exact setup checklist in order, including how I revoke your access in one step if I ever need to. Don't ask me to paste any key or password here.
  • ✓Key pair created; passwordless login tested in a second window
  • ✓Password authentication disabled on the server
  • ✓Dedicated non-root user for Muse; revoke step written down
Step 7

Give it a home — the always-on server stack

With the box from Step 1 and keys from Step 6, you assemble the home base. Four layers, in this order:

1. Tailscale — your private network

Install Tailscale on the server, your phone, and your laptop. Every device gets a private address and can reach the others from anywhere — no port forwarding, no exposed services. This is how Muse's infrastructure, your servers, and you all find each other safely.

curl -fsSL https://tailscale.com/install.sh | sh sudo tailscale up

2. Docker — the app shelf

Docker runs each service in its own container: install, update, or delete without breaking the neighbors. The starter stack most homes end up with:

ServiceWhy it's there
n8nVisual automations — “when this happens, do that” across hundreds of apps, no code
OllamaLocal AI models on your hardware — private, free per use, the engine for local agents (Step 12)
A databasePostgres or SQLite for the apps Muse builds (Step 9)
Your appsWhatever Muse builds for you, containerized and restart-proof
A reverse proxyOne front door with HTTPS for everything on the box

3. n8n — automations you can see

Three starter flows cover half of home-and-business automation: (1) new form submission → email + spreadsheet row + text message; (2) nightly backup check → alert only if something failed; (3) RSS/web watcher → morning summary. Ask Muse to design the flow, then import and test it with you.

4. Backups — the layer everyone skips once

Nightly, automatic, and off the box: database dumps and data folders copied to a second drive or a cloud bucket. A backup you've never restored is a rumor — do one test restore in the first month.

The cPanel workflow, step by step

  1. Point the domain. Register or transfer the domain, set nameservers/DNS as your welcome email specifies, and wait for propagation (usually under an hour).
  2. Create the site in cPanel. Domains → Create a New Domain for each site or subdomain. Each gets its own document root folder — keep them separate from day one.
  3. Turn on SSL. Security → SSL/TLS → run AutoSSL (Let's Encrypt). Never launch a site that says “Not Secure.”
  4. Deploy the site. Three routes: one-click installer (WordPress etc.) from the apps library; File Manager upload for a static site Muse built; or Git deployment for apps under active development.
  5. Email on the domain. Email Accounts → create you@yourdomain.com, set up the phone app or webmail, and add the SPF/DKIM records cPanel generates so your mail lands in inboxes, not spam.

Step 9

Databases — where your apps keep the truth

A website shows things; a database remembers them. Customers, orders, bookings, inventory — if it must survive a restart, it lives in a database. Muse designs the tables; you approve what gets remembered.

Pick the right database

DatabaseUse it when
SQLiteOne app, modest data, zero administration. A single file — back it up by copying it. Perfect for tools Muse builds at home.
MySQL / MariaDBcPanel hosting, WordPress and most one-click apps. Created in cPanel → MySQL Databases, with its own user per app.
PostgreSQLSerious apps on your server or VPS — the default choice for new Node builds (Step 10).

Create one in cPanel (the pattern never changes)

  1. cPanel → MySQL Databases → create the database (note the prefixed name, e.g. acct_shopdb).
  2. Create a user with a generated password — store it in the app's config on the server, never in chat or email.
  3. Add the user to the database with the privileges the app needs — most apps want ALL on their own database only.
  4. Put the four values (host, database, user, password) in the app's .env or config file. That's the whole handshake.
Design the database for [app idea]. List the tables and what each one stores, in plain English, and flag anything personal or sensitive we'd be keeping. Keep it as simple as possible — I'd rather add a table later than overbuild now.
Backups, again — because databases are the crown jewelscPanel → Backup gives you one-click database exports; on your own server, a nightly mysqldump/pg_dump cron job does the same. Schedule it the day the database is born, and keep copies off the machine the database lives on.
  • ✓Database type chosen for a reason you can state
  • ✓One user per app; credentials in server config only
  • ✓Nightly export/dump scheduled off-box
Step 10

Node apps in production — beyond static pages

When a site needs logins, payments, or live data, it becomes a Node.js app: a program that runs continuously and answers requests. Muse can write it, deploy it, and keep it alive — here's the shape of doing it right, on cPanel or your own server.

On HyndSyte cPanel hosting

  1. cPanel → Setup Node.js App → Create Application: pick the Node version, set the application root (its folder) and the startup file (usually app.js).
  2. Upload the app (File Manager or Git), then click Run NPM Install in the same screen to install its dependencies.
  3. Add environment variables in the Node screen — database credentials, API keys — never hard-code them in the code.
  4. Restart the app after every change; cPanel's Passenger layer serves it on your domain with the SSL from Step 8.

On your own server (Docker route)

FROM node:22-alpine WORKDIR /app COPY package*.json ./ RUN npm ci --omit=dev COPY . . CMD ["node", "app.js"]

One container per app, a process manager or Docker's restart policy keeping it up, logs flowing somewhere you can read. Muse writes the Dockerfile; you approve what it exposes.

The production checklist Muse should never skip

  • .env for secrets — and .env never committed to Git.
  • Health check — a /health route that answers OK, so watchers (Step 11) can tell alive from dead.
  • Logs — errors written to a file or service, reviewed weekly.
  • Backups wired — its database is in the nightly job from Step 9 before the first real user arrives.
Turn the tool you built me into a proper Node app I can run in production: environment variables for secrets, a /health route, logging, and a restart-safe setup.   • ✓Secrets in environment variables only

Step 11

Schedules, watchers & autopilot

This is where the whole build pays off. Everything above was capability; this step is initiative — work that happens because past-you scheduled it.

MechanismWhat it doesExample
Scheduled jobsRun a task on a clockWeekday 6:30 AM briefing: calendar, weather, overnight email, today's priorities
Watchers (hooks)React when something happensNew lead email arrives → draft a reply and flag you; camera sees a person while you're away → alert with the snapshot
GoalsLong-running efforts with their own memory“Grow the shop to 200 customers” — Muse tracks progress and works it between your conversations
Server cronThe machine-level classicsBackups, log rotation, health checks on every service from Step 7

The autopilot rules

  • Start silent. New automations report what they would do for the first week. Promote them to acting once the reports are boring.
  • Alert on exceptions, not on success. “Backup failed” is a message. “Backup succeeded” 365 times is noise that trains you to ignore the failure.
  • Every automation gets an owner and an off switch — you, and a named way to stop it.
  • Review monthly. Ten minutes: what's running, what's it done, what should die.
Turn my morning routine into a scheduled briefing: every weekday at 6:30 AM, summarize my calendar, flag urgent overnight email, check that last night's server backups succeeded, and give me the day's top three priorities. For the first week, prepare it but don't send anything anywhere — just have it ready here.
  • ✓One scheduled briefing running
  • ✓One watcher live, in report-only mode
  • ✓Monthly automation review on the calendar
Step 12

Advanced — local agents, voice, and running a business on it

Local agents on your own hardware

With Ollama on the home server (Step 7), Muse can delegate bulk work to local models: classify 500 emails, summarize documents, draft first passes — private, offline-capable, and free per use. The pattern that works: local agents do volume, Muse does judgment. Anything a local model produces that leaves the building gets Muse's review first.

Design a local-agent setup for my server: which model for inbox classification, which for document summaries, and how you'll review their output before anything is sent or filed. Include the guardrails — what local agents may never do unsupervised.

Voice — Muse that talks and answers the phone

  • Read-aloud & voice notes: briefings as audio for the drive; dictated brain-dumps transcribed into plans.
  • A real phone number: through a voice provider, Muse can place and take calls — appointment reminders, follow-ups, after-hours answering with a transcript in your inbox by morning.
  • House voice: wake-word satellites in rooms, tied to your home automation — “lights, status, who's home” without a phone in hand.

Its own inbox — Muse as staff

Give Muse a business email address of its own. With rules you set, it answers routine questions (pricing, hours, scheduling) directly, drafts quotes for your approval, and escalates anything committing you — money, contracts, disputes — with a draft attached. Customers get fast answers; you keep the pen on everything that matters.

Running the business on autopilot

Assembled, a normal day looks like this: the 6:30 briefing lands before you're up; leads that arrived overnight have draft replies waiting; the shop's schedule, invoices, and follow-ups are tended by watchers; your sites and apps are monitored, backed up, and patched on the home stack and HyndSyte hosting alike; and your one job is the approval queue — the short list of things Muse prepared but won't do without your word. That's the OpenClaw you wished you'd had a year ago, except it answers to you, lives on your hardware, and keeps the keys where you can see them.
